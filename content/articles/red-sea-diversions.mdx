A container service is a loop. A fixed number of ships, spaced a week apart, cycle between a set of ports. Add ten days to the loop and either the service needs more ships to hold weekly frequency, or the frequency slips. In late 2023 and 2024 the lines chose the first option, and pulled capacity from everywhere else to do it.

## Rates followed the ships, not the risk

The Shanghai index (<Sym s="SCFI" />) tripled in the first weeks and stayed elevated for months. It is tempting to read that as a risk premium. It was mostly a capacity effect: the same cargo, the same ships, a longer loop. When the market later softened, it was because newbuild deliveries — the largest orderbook in the industry's history — caught up with the longer loops.

## Who kept going

Not everyone rerouted. Tankers with certain flags, some bulk carriers, and ships whose owners judged the risk differently continued to transit. The war-risk premium (<Sym s="WAR.RS" />) — quoted as a percentage of hull value for a single voyage — became the price of that judgement, and it moved with each incident rather than with any index.

<Callout title="Why a shipping story is also a defence story">A merchant fleet that reroutes around a chokepoint is doing exactly what a naval planner would call a denial effect. No ships were sunk in numbers that mattered; the sea lane was still closed in every commercial sense. That is the dual-use point this journal keeps returning to: the same hulls, the same lanes, read two ways.</Callout>

## What to watch now

The transit count at Bab el-Mandeb (<Sym s="BAB.TX" />) is the cleanest single indicator. Rates lag it. Insurance leads it. When the count recovers and the premium falls for more than a few weeks, the loops will shorten again — and the capacity that returns will land on a market that has been building ships for the long route.
