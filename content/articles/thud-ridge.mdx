The Republic F-105 Thunderchief was never meant to do what it became famous for. Designed in the early 1950s as a single-seat, single-engine strike fighter, its job was to carry one nuclear weapon in an internal bay, low and fast, to a target in Eastern Europe. Everything about the airframe followed from that: the swept wing with forward-hinged intakes, the enormous Pratt & Whitney J75, the fuselage bay that was later plated over and filled with fuel.

## The ridge

Pilots inbound to the Hanoi area from Thailand learned to fly the long, north-west-to-south-east ridgeline that pointed at the capital like a gunsight. They called it Thud Ridge. It gave a hard navigation reference, some terrain masking from the radars in the valley, and a landmark to run back to when things went wrong. Jack Broughton's 1969 memoir made the name permanent, and gave the aircraft its identity: not the sleekest, not the most agile, but the one that went downtown day after day.

<PullQuote cite="Col. Jack Broughton, 355th TFW">You could go faster in a Thud on the deck than anything else in the war, and you often needed to.</PullQuote>

## Why the losses were so heavy

The F-105 flew most of the Air Force's strike sorties against North Vietnam in the Rolling Thunder years. The targets — bridges, rail yards, the Thai Nguyen steel works — sat inside the densest integrated air defence anyone had yet attacked: radar-directed anti-aircraft artillery, SA-2 Guideline missiles, and MiG-17s and MiG-21s vectored from the ground. The tactics of 1965 asked a big, fast, non-manoeuvrable aircraft to fly predictable routes at medium altitude into that. Losses were, by any modern standard, unsustainable: several hundred of the roughly 800 airframes built were lost in Southeast Asia, the great majority to ground fire rather than fighters.

## What the Thud left behind

Two things. First, the Wild Weasel mission. Two-seat F-105Fs and the later G models, fitted with radar-homing receivers and Shrike and Standard ARM missiles, went in ahead of the strike package to find and hit the SA-2 sites. The doctrine — first in, last out, "YGBSM" — is the direct ancestor of every SEAD unit since.

Second, an argument about aircraft design that has never stopped. The F-105's failure was not the aircraft; it was the mismatch between a platform built for one war and the war it was sent to fight. The next generation — the F-4 in the near term, the F-15 and F-16 later — was shaped by that lesson as much as by any threat study.

<Dinkus />

Today the Thunderchief is a museum piece and a call-sign. But the shape of the problem it exposed — a heavy, expensive platform sent repeatedly against a maturing defence — is the shape of a lot of current debates about air power, and about the survivability of everything from tankers to merchant ships. Which is why it opens this journal.
